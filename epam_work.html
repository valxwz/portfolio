A Quick Glance

The Ask

Our client is a well-established global logistics company with over 50 years in operation. They wanted us to redesign their core tracking tool as a gradual, 20–30 year vision — credible enough to win budget from the board.

Starting Hypothesis

Users need a predictive tracking with AI, plus a better shipment tracking info presentation so it doesn't need explaining.

Still Open

Why staff run into shipment tracking issues and what's actually blocking them.

Outcome

Redesigned the client's tracking tool to show not just each shipment movement, but a summary that explains what those updates mean for the user.

Design as Alignment, Not a Spec

This wasn't a dev-handoff project. The leadership board needed a vision credible enough to approve budget on a 20–30 year horizon — so design was the tool for asking questions and getting senior stakeholders to align, not for producing specs.

Why I Want to Talk About it

What makes this project special isn't the pretty visuals we delivered — I mean, the deliverables are sleek, and I'm proud of my team for that. But the highlight I want to talk about is how we managed alignment, both with the client and within the team: alignment breaks under pressure, and someone has to make the call. Two moments show what that looked like in practice:

1

A solo call under deadline - during mid term

With the team on leave over a holiday week and a client workshop looming, I had to make the call alone and get the team's buy-in fast: redesign into one unified product, or split into several for the diverging user needs. I proposed going with one — new enough that the client would feel their investment was worth it, familiar enough they could still map it to what they knew — and used the workshop to test that bet directly.

2

A late requirement mismatch - during the final phase

Near the end, we learned the client expected the prototype to match use-case scenarios that had never reached the design team — a surprise to everyone, including our PM. Instead of untangling blame, we ran a quick three-way call with the business analyst and the client's product owner to lock the actual scope, and we delivered what we'd promised.

Another special part is that no single designer owned this end to end — we all built on each other's work. My part was closer to a thread-holder for the tracking-visualization feature: I didn't own every detail, but I made the calls on requirements and matched the other two designers to their strengths. I'd initiate the design, they'd build on it as my sounding board, and I'd take it back to iterate. As the one presenting to the board, I made sure it all read as one story.

The Problem

Early research revealed a sharper problem than the one we'd been hired to solve: visibility into shipment information wasn't actually the issue — the data on what happened to a shipment already existed in abundance. The real problem was that operations and customer service staff couldn't quickly determine a shipment's health, what was blocking it, who owned the next step, or what to do about it. That information existed, but it was scattered across multiple internal systems and buried in cryptic operational codes. So staff built their own workarounds — calls, emails, tribal knowledge — to piece together an answer.

One early finding shaped the whole direction: showing the latest shipment event wasn't what mattered to users — knowing the next step and the deadline was. That's what pushed the concept from a status feed to an action-first quick-glance.

The Deliverable

An AI quick-glance feature for internal ops and customer service staff — distilling fragmented, jargon-heavy shipment data into an instant, plain-language read on a shipment's status. We also flagged the gaps the client still needs to close — ones outside our project timeline, but necessary before this solution could actually be built.

Before

Scattered systems, cryptic codes, and cross-team calls or manual lookups just to piece together a shipment's status.

After

AI-powered quick Glance of one instant, plain-language read - what's happening, what's blocking it, and what to do next.

The output moved to board review — stakeholders rated the engagement 9/10 and wrote testimony for future work together.

The Starting Point

We worked on a pre-sale project to build a fully interactive prototype as a proof of concept — a working chatbot paired with its admin platform. I started with almost nothing to go on: just two tender specification documents from the client and no direct access to end users or product owners.

A Three-Step Design Process

This is my AI-assisted design process — not a single linear pass, but a three-step loop that repeated every few days, once per feature.

1

Claude as a research partner

I fed Claude the two raw specs and asked it to translate documentation into user goals, scenarios, use cases, and stories. I need to know what matter to the user, why they matter, and what actions the user is trying to take.

2

Organize my design requirements in Cowork

Once I understood the scenario, I described what we wanted to build in plain language. Claude structured it into complete, gap-checked design requirements. I also drafted and visually tested solutions before touching implementation.

3

Implementation

Once I'm satisfied with the design, I move on to Code to build it on my local and show my ideas to my team. Once we are all agree with the solution, I commit my changes to the repository.

The Outcome

The prototype came together almost entirely through vibe-coding. Figma became the tool to literally draw boxes for a quick visual sync with the team. I leaned on AI to fill in my gaps in chatbot-admin domain knowledge, so I could define the right user problem from the start. Vibe-coding let me prototype fast and accurately, which meant covering the edge cases too — not just the happy path. The result: a prototype the Request for Proposal (RFP) team felt confident bringing straight into the pitch to win the client.
